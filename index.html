MUSIC 3460 - Log Driven Sonification System

Download Project Slideshow (PDF)
DescriptionThis project turns system log files into a generative soundscape, where routine system activity produces ambient textures and errors produce audible disruptions. The idea came from combining my cybersecurity background with sound design, with CDC (cyber defense competition) environments as a possible use case: making system activity audible in real time as another layer of monitoring, alongside visual tools.

The patch reads in a .txt or .log file, validates the extension, and steps through it line by line on a user-controlled clock. A set of pattern-matching objects scans each line for keywords such as CRON, FAIL, Timed out, Starting/Started, Finished, DENIED, root, and refresh, and routes each match to its own subpatch. Each event type gets a distinct sound: CRON events become rhythmic kick-and-hi-hat patterns, Start/Timed-out events become shifting wind-like tones, Finished events become a short decaying sparkle, DENIED events become a harsh beep that grows louder with repetition, and ongoing background activity produces wind-chime and rain-like ambient textures. A simple visual display shows the three most recent log lines as they're read.

The design was shaped by research into data sonification and generative music, particularly Brian Eno's idea of music as a living system that evolves from constraints rather than direct composition, and Eric Tamm's writing on Eno's use of layered, independent voices. That led me to build each sound as its own self-contained subpatch, so the different log events combine into one evolving texture rather than a fixed, pre-written piece.
Your roleThis was an individual project. I designed and built the full system alone: the file input and validation, the log-parsing and keyword-matching logic, the line-by-line playback and display, and all seven sound subpatches.
Skills or knowledge gainedMax/MSP patching, real-time text parsing and pattern matching with regex, synthesis and sound design, applying sonification concepts to real datasets, research into generative music theory and its application to interactive systems.
Resources usedMax/MSP (Max 9), sample and generated system log files, research sources: Brian Eno's writing on generative music, Eric Tamm's book "Brian Eno: His Music and the Vertical Color of Sound", and Miranda and Wanderley's work on data-driven musical controllers.

CPRE 3810 - RISC-V Processor Design (Single-Cycle, Pipelined, Hazard-Resolved)

DescriptionOver a semester, in a partner based project, we designed and built three working iterations of RISC-V processors in structural VHDL:
  • Single-Cycle Processor: A complete datapath and control unit supporting the full RISC-V base instruction set including arithmetic, logical, shifts, loads/stores, and all control flow instructions. I designed the ALU with a barrel shifter, the control logic, and the instruction fetch/brach logic. The final synthesis of this design showed a maximum frequency of about 22 MHz since it was limited by a long critical path through the load instruction datapath.
  • Software-scheduled Pipelined Processor: The same datapath was split into five stages: fetch, decode, execute, memory, and writeback. These stages were connected by pipeline registers with no hazard detection. Hazards were avoided by manually inserting NOPs in the assembly code. I designed the hazard-free control logic and the instruction fetch/brach logic. This raised the clock frequency to about 60 MHz, but the NOPs made up more than half of the instructions in our larger test programs.
  • Hardware-Scheduled Pipelined Processor: We added forwarding, stalling, and flush logic to the Software-scheduled design so that unmodified programs would run correctly without manual NOP instructions. I worked out which data and control hazards needed stalls versus forwarding, and implemented the flush logic for branches and jumps.
    • All of these designs were tested with a synthetic instruction-coverage program, a provided Grendel program benchmark, and a recursive merge sort. They were analyzed for the number of instructions executed, clock cycles, CPI, and total execution time. The hardware-scheduled design was the fastest overall, since dynamic hazard handling avoided the instruction-count bloat that the software-scheduled design suffered from.
Your roleI work as one of two team members on all three parts of this project. I was the primary designer of the ALU (including the barrel shifter), the control unit, and the fetch/branch logic in the single-cycle processor. I led the hazard analysis and flush/stall logic for the hardware-scheduled pipeline. I wrote individual testbenches for my modules in QuestaSim before full integration, and contributed to the final performance analysis and report.
Skills or knowledge gainedStructural VHDL design, modular hardware design, processor architecture, hardware verification with QuestaSim waveform testing, RISC-V assembly, synthesis and performance analysis.
Resources usedQuestaSim, RISC-V Reference Manual, RARS simulator, course-provided test framework and skeleton code, Intel/Altera synthesis tools.

EE 4524 - Microcontroller Projects (ATmega4809)

DescriptionWhile abroad I took a course that was equivalent to CPRE 2880 and worked on a couple of embedded systems projects built in C on an ATmega4809 microcontroller. The first covers timers, the ADC, and interrupts. The second builds on the first project and adds input capture, the event system, and a serial interface.

Project 1: Interrupt-Driven LED Display. This program that runs several tasks at once with no delay loops. A timer interrupt sweeps a single LED across a 10-LED array (Cylon eyes), and the ADC reads a potentiometer to set the sweep speed. A push button switches the array to a voltage bar graph. In the Advanced task, the real-time counter alternates every 16 seconds between the normal display and a split display, with the Cylon pattern and the bar graph shown together.

Project 2: Multi-Sensor Serial Control System. This program is controlled by single-character commands over a serial terminal. It moves a servo at user-selected speeds, or has it follow a potentiometer. It measures distance with an ultrasonic sensor and reports it in millimetres. It measures the period and pulse widths of a 555 timer signal and detects when that signal stops. It also reports the ADC voltage in millivolts. Hardware timers, the event system, and interrupts handle the measurements, with integer-only arithmetic.
My roleI was the primary developer on both projects, working with a lab partner. I wrote and debugged most of each program, including the timer, ADC, and RTC setup for Project 1, and the peripheral setup, interrupt routines, and serial command interface for Project 2. I also completed the Advanced task in Project 1 and the challenge feature in Project 2, detecting a stopped 555 timer.
Skills or knowledge gainedRegister-level embedded C, hardware timers (PWM, pulse-width and frequency capture), ADC and RTC configuration, event system routing, interrupt-driven design, UART communication, hardware debugging.
Resources usedATmega4809 datasheet, Arduino Nano Every, lab shield, HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor, micro servo, 555 timer circuit, serial terminal, Microchip (Atmel) Studio.

CPRE 1860 - Gesture Master

Download Project Slideshow (PDF)
DescriptionGesture master is a wearable, glove-based device that recognizes hand motions and reponds with a short message. Our team of four, designed and built this device to see how hardware and software could work together to solve a real-world problem.

We utilized an Adafruit BNO055 gyroscope and accelerometer sensor wired to an Arduino Uno. The sensor reports acceleration, orientation, and temperature data to the Arduino. Our program check the sensor connection in setup, then runs a loop that converts the x, y, and z readings into an orientaton that tracks how long a motion lasts. The current POC can identify three gestures: stop, wave, and punch.
  • Stop: A raised palm held still, which prints a message and ends the program.
  • Wave hello: A left-to-right or right-to-left wave.
  • Punch: A strictly forward punch.
The goal for this project was to support the idea that this approach could be taken beyond the classroom and support gesture control for people wiht disabilities, hands-free interaction with digital devices, healthcare technology for people with limited mobility, and interactive learning or entertainment.
My roleI worked mainly on the software. I wrote the first direction methods in the Arduino IDE with a teammate (up, down, left, and right), and we later combined them into a single orientation method. I helped program our first detected gesture, stop, and then worked with a teammate to finalize the stop, wave, and punch gestures. I also designed the software UML diagram, since I had the best grasp of how the code fit together, and helped polish our final presentation.
Skills or knowledge gainedI learned about sensor hardware and wiring, soldering, reading and interpreting sensor data in the Arduino IDE, analyzing vector data for accuracy, designing software with UML diagrams, and collaborating in a team.
Resources usedAdafruit BNO055 sensor, Arduino Uno, Arduino IDE, and Adafruit's documentation for wiring, IDE setup, and the Unified Sensor Driver.
